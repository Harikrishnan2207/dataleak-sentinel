# Detection evaluation on labelled synthetic scenarios

Generated by `python -m sentinel.evaluation`. The numbers are deterministic: the same code and seeds reproduce this file exactly. **Everything here is synthetic.** It measures how the rules behave on data designed by the author, not how they would perform against real users or real attackers.

## How the test works

- **30 synthetic organisations** (seeds 1000 to 1029). Each has 40 users in three profiles (15 light readers with a typical size near 3 rows, 15 typical near 7, 10 heavy analysts near 27) and 14 days of ordinary activity (about 2,414 events per organisation).
- **Attack episodes** (3 per type per organisation, each on a different user who also has normal history):
  - `bulk_dump`: one huge pull (800 to 20,000 rows).
  - `repeated_pulls`: 5 pulls of 60 to 300 rows, 6 to 12 minutes apart.
  - `rapid_burst`: 18 small queries (3 to 12 rows), 20 to 60 s apart.
  - `low_volume_spike`: a steady light user suddenly reads 12 to 19 rows (inside the Low band).
  - `sub_threshold_drip`: 25 queries of 14 to 20 rows, 15 to 40 minutes apart (built to stay under every threshold).
  - `gradual_ramp`: 20 queries over 5.5 days growing from the user's normal size to about 90 rows.
- **Benign activity**, including deliberately awkward cases that should cause false alerts:
  - `ordinary`: everyday activity with each user's own typical size.
  - `legit_export`: a legitimate weekly export of 150 to 400 rows.
  - `legit_burst`: a legitimate burst of 10 to 14 small lookups within about 8 minutes.
- **Ground truth** comes from how each event was generated. An event is *malicious* if it belongs to an attack episode, and *benign* otherwise. The detector never sees these labels.
- **A flagged event** is one scored Medium or High (the system raises an alert for these). An attack episode is **detected** if at least one of its events is flagged.
- **Three configurations** show what each layer adds: volume thresholds only; volume plus the behaviour rules (repeated access, frequency, cumulative volume); and the full engine with the per-user anomaly rule, using the shipped default settings.
- **Development tuning:** the current defaults (12-hour cumulative window, 400-record cumulative limit, and robust anomaly z-score 2.5) were selected after inspecting results on this benchmark. These figures are useful for regression and trade-off tracking, not independent validation.

## What the numbers show

- Every attack type was detected in every episode by the full engine on this data.
- **False alerts:** 17% of `ordinary` events were flagged (everyday activity with each user's own typical size). The rules cannot tell this legitimate activity from an attack.
- **False alerts:** 100% of `legit_export` events were flagged (a legitimate weekly export of 150 to 400 rows). The rules cannot tell this legitimate activity from an attack.
- **False alerts:** 26% of `legit_burst` events were flagged (a legitimate burst of 10 to 14 small lookups within about 8 minutes). The rules cannot tell this legitimate activity from an attack.
- Legitimate exports remain indistinguishable from unauthorized exports because events do not carry trusted approval or workload context; the evaluation flags all labelled legitimate exports.
- The per-user anomaly rule changed detection of `low_volume_spike` from 0% to 100%.
- Cost of the anomaly rule: false alerts among benign events went from 17.45% to 17.67%.
- **Main cause of false alerts:** `heavy` users had 67% of their ordinary events flagged. Their typical size is about 27 rows, against a fixed Low limit of 20. Fixed global thresholds cannot adapt to users whose normal volume is above them, and the per-user anomaly rule only adds alerts, it never lowers them.
- `rapid_burst` was always detected, but only after a median of 9 attack events had already happened.
- `sub_threshold_drip` was always detected, but only after a median of 20 attack events had already happened.
- `gradual_ramp` was always detected, but only after a median of 7 attack events had already happened.
- Volume thresholds alone detected 50% of attack episodes, the full engine 100%.

## Limits of this evaluation

- The data, the attacks and the labels were all written by the same person who wrote the detector. Good results here show the rules do what they were designed to do, not that they would catch real attackers.
- Attack types reflect assumptions about what insiders do. Real attackers can behave differently, and the `sub_threshold_drip` and `gradual_ramp` cases show that an attacker who knows the thresholds can avoid them.
- The headline false alert rate depends on a choice made by the author: a quarter of the users are "heavy" analysts whose normal reads exceed the 20-row Low limit. With fewer such users the rate would be much lower; with more it would be higher. Read the by-profile table, not just the total.
- The legitimate-activity cases are few and simple. Real workloads (month-end reports, batch jobs, new starters with no history) would produce more and different false alerts.
- Precision depends on how common attacks are. Here 8.6% of events are malicious, far more than in real systems, so real precision would be lower for the same false alert rate.
- Episodes within one organisation are not independent samples of the real world, so the spread between seeds shows generator variation, not a confidence interval for real performance.
- Detection happens after the access. The system raises alerts; it does not block queries.
- Reproduce with `python -m sentinel.evaluation` (takes a few seconds). Changing the code, the seeds or the settings changes the numbers.
